From 28 October · Research before anything else

I left my job at the end of October and started the search on 28 October. For the first three days I did not apply anywhere. AI agents researched in parallel while I made decisions: what roles exist for someone with my background, how applicant tracking systems filter people, where openings are actually published, which companies in Barcelona fit, how the market changed since 2022, what a CV should look like in 2026. More than twenty reports in the first three days.

The research did not stop there. By 8 November there were about sixty reports: how CVs are read in the age of AI, how screening really works, the AI tools recruiters use, which messenger channels carry real openings, how old a posting can be and still be worth it, and talks by hiring people on video, each claim checked against other sources and marked as applicable to me or not. Many of the rules in CTS and in my CV trace back to one of these reports.

Decisions from that phase: a wide funnel (startups and large companies, mobile and beyond), my own constraints written down as hard rules, a CV rebuilt from scratch, and my own system instead of an existing one.

30 October – 6 November · People and events

The first full week went mostly into people. I rebuilt my LinkedIn profile, registered for every relevant meetup and conference in Barcelona for the coming weeks, printed cards with a QR code, introduced myself in local tech communities, and wrote to former colleagues and friends in other companies. I went to three events that week: an AI engineers meetup, a Claude Code meetup and a talk on hiring in the age of AI. Every contact and every touch went into CTS.

2–8 November · Building the machine

In parallel CTS grew from a calendar into a search system. On 2 November it read career pages for the first time and brought in 14,000 postings. In the following days: the CV became something assembled from approved blocks and tested against real parsers; email alerts, job boards and messenger channels joined as sources; the rules learned from my own skip decisions; and on 8 November an A/B experiment started on the CV itself.

From 9 November · Applying at volume

The first applications went out on 5 November, a couple a day. On 7 November I set a daily target of 50 and a counter for it in CTS. From 9 November applying became the main activity: 24 that day, then 14, 28, 8, 10. Still manual, only faster: CTS orders the queue, builds the CV, and an agent fills the form, but every application waits for my review and go-ahead. I have not reached 50 a day yet. It is an ambitious number, and I keep it as a target anyway.

11 November · Narrowing the direction

With real data in hand I made the decisions I had postponed: AI engineering became the target track, with mobile staying as the fast route; engineering management is not my track; and LinkedIn stays manual. The scoring got a second opinion: a small decision model, calibrated on openings I had labelled myself.

13 November · Review in one sitting, and first numbers

Until now each form waited in its own tab for me to press Submit. Now I can pick openings and start applications from my phone, let the agents prepare everything, and review all the forms later at home in one go: by voice or text I say what to change and which ones to send. The agent that filled the form makes the changes and sends it only after my explicit go-ahead for that application.

The funnel so far: 88 applications, 56 confirmed by the company, 16 replies, 5 screening calls, no interviews yet.

  • 13 November 2026

What I expect

From what the market looks like now, my rough expectation is an offer somewhere between 300 and 600 applications. It may well take more. At the current pace that is weeks, not days, which is why the system matters more than any single application.
